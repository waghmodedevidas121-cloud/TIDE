Phase 0 — Design Review

TIDE

An aggressive pre-production critique of the tide-based territory mechanic, benchmarked against the genre it borrows from, with a phased roadmap and defined kill-tests.

No code written TypeScript Phaser 3 WebGL Desktop + Mobile Browser Status: awaiting approval

00 Verdict

The short version

The mechanic as described in the brief is weak — too vague, and one optimization away from being invisible. Sharpened into the specific ruleset below, it is strong: it adds a temporal decision axis to a genre that has run on spatial decisions for eight years, and it hands us an offense verb nobody in the genre has.

Recommendation: build the sharpened version. Three alternatives are documented in section 10 for comparison. The tide's fate is settled by a kill-test defined in advance in section 6 — not by opinion, and not later.

01 The concept, stated precisely

The brief says "water/tide-based territory mechanic," which is too vague to build or critique. Here is the specific reading this document reviews:

You are a boat on a grid of seabed with fixed per-cell elevation. A global water level rises and falls on a visible clock — roughly a 40-second period, split into 20s EBB (falling) and 20s FLOOD (rising).

High tide (FLOOD): shallow reef flats submerge, giving wide open water, a fast boat, and long sightlines. But a strong current pushes your hull and the tide erodes your claimed edges. Capturing now scores a 1.6× Surge multiplier.

Low tide (EBB): flats dry into impassable land. Water contracts into deep channels — slow boat, narrow corridors, ambush country. Your territory locks and hardens; nothing erodes.

Trails behave like Paper.io: loop back into your own water to bank the enclosed seabed as yours. Score is a round score, not a lifetime score.

The thesis: Paper.io is a spatial problem — where do I loop. TIDE makes it a spatial + temporal problem — where do I loop, given what the water is about to do. That single addition is the entire product, so it has to carry the entire product. Section 6 is where we find out whether it does.

02 Core gameplay loop

Second-by-second — roughly 8 seconds per cycle

Read the tide clock → commit to a channel → lay trail → watch for interception → close the loop → bank the area → immediately reposition, because the water is about to change underneath you.

Round — 3 minutes

  • Open on EBB — safe, cramped, a quiet chance to learn the map.
  • First FLOOD — the greed window opens. Everybody surges. Land grows fast and starts bleeding.
  • EBB — hold and consolidate. What you kept is now hardened and permanent.
  • FLOOD — erosion bites. The leader's fragile holdings strip away first.
  • Final FLOOD — the round is decided by who over-extended.

Meta loop

You lose score, not just a life. A greedy flood-phase run shows a huge number that then visibly erodes away on screen. Watching your own 4,200 collapse to 2,900 is the emotional beat that makes you press play again. Nothing else in this genre produces that moment — they produce a death screen.

03 Risk / reward — three axes instead of one

Paper.io has two: loop size and route safety. TIDE adds a third and makes the first two conditional on it.

 Low tide — EBBHigh tide — FLOOD

Boat speedSlowFast
CurrentWeakStrong — must counter-steer
Your territoryLocked & hardenedActively eroding
Dominant riskAmbush in narrow corridorsCut off, washed out, edges stripped

Erosion as offense

This is the piece that gives TIDE something Paper.io never had. In Paper.io the only way to hurt a player is to physically cut their trail. In TIDE you can hurt a player by surviving while they over-extend.

Aggression becomes patience — a far fresher aggressive archetype than "cut them off," and it means the leader is attackable without the leader ever making a mistake.

Skill expression, ranked by depth

  1. Counter-steering the current. Pure execution. Learnable in 30 seconds, and it separates players within the first minute.
  2. Timing the loop. Knowing a fourteen-second window exists and that your loop must complete before the turn.
  3. Territory geometry. Thin margins die first under erosion, so compact blobs beat sprawling webs. This rewards planning and quietly punishes the greedy beginner — the best kind of lesson, because it teaches without a tutorial.
  4. Routeing under threat. Reading other boats' trails and predicting where they intend to close.

Four axes with genuine headroom. Paper.io really only has the fourth and a fragment of the third.

04 Replayability factors

Structural

  • 3-minute rounds, ~2s respawn. Zero downtime. This alone fixes Paper.io's worst trait: a leaderboard game where dying costs you five minutes of boredom.
  • A decision every 20 seconds, not just every run — the tide forces re-evaluation mid-run.
  • Real comebacks. Erosion churns the leaderboard on its own, so the leader is never safe and anyone can win.

Content economy

  • Cheap variety. Tide amplitude, period, phase offset, map seed, bot count — these are tables, not assets.
  • Readable skill gap. A noob sees a noob's flood gamble collapse. An expert sees exactly why. Same round, two levels of comprehension.

05 Comparison with the genre

GameWhat TIDE takesWhat TIDE takes that it lacks
Paper.io The enclose-a-loop verb, the grid, and above all its legibility target — one glance tells you everything. TIDE aims at that same bar, which is exactly why the tide must be a global overlay and never per-cell state. A clock. An erosion mechanic. Offense-by-patience.
Paper.io's weakness we exploit: its meta is flat. Optimal play is "grow huge, then turtle forever," so the skill ceiling is route optimization and not much else.
Hole.io The short round, frictionless onboarding, constant escalation, and the no-punishment feel of losing — you just stop growing rather than rage-quitting. Death. Real stakes. A lethal, forecastable global event.
Hole.io's weakness we exploit: it has zero risk. You cannot die. TIDE restores death and makes the tide the thing that kills you.
Agar.io Genuine PvP stakes, comeback potential, and a skill ceiling worth climbing. Territory itself. Agar.io is a presence game — mass as power, no claims. TIDE is the opposite bet: control, not consumption. That also reads better on a phone, because a filled region is legible and a big dot is not.
Snake.io Instant, pre-verbal readability of self-danger. You see your own trail and you know. TIDE must earn that same clarity for territory edges, not just the trail.
Snake.io's weakness: no territory, low depth, becomes solved, feels cheap.
The gap nobody occupies

All four are spatial-only games. Not one of them makes the player manage a global clock that is simultaneously a scoring opportunity, a survival threat, and a weapon aimed at the leader. That is the space TIDE is built for.

06 Why it could fail

This is the section that matters most. Eight ways this dies, ordered by likelihood.

Failure 1 — most likely, and fatal

The tide becomes a waiting room. On its face the brief's mechanic is decorative. A player who notices "land is safer" will simply sit in EBB, expand only in the calm, and never sail during FLOOD. If that player is right, we have built Paper.io with a progress bar.

Everything in this document exists to prevent that, and prevention is not free. It requires the Surge multiplier to be genuinely tempting, the current to be a skill test rather than a punishment, and EBB to be cramped enough that turtling is uncomfortable.

Kill-test — agreed now, so it cannot be rationalised away later

In a five-minute playtest, if a competent player never deliberately expands during FLOOD for the multiplier, the tide is decoration and we cut it. I am committing to this test before any code exists, because it is precisely the kind of decision that gets quietly reversed once implementation is 80% done and everyone is tired.

Failure 2 — legibility collapse

Territory + trail + tide is three overlapping visual languages. Paper.io manages two and is already straining. This is the risk I take most seriously, and it is a rendering-architecture problem rather than a gameplay one: the tide must be a single global translucent water sheet plus a moving shoreline band, drawn over the grid — never as per-cell state. If any cell is ever individually "wet" or "dry," we have lost. You must never have to ask whether a cell is submerged. You ask whether a cell is yours.

Failure 3 — erosion feels like theft

If the tide silently eats your land, players will blame the game rather than their own greed. Erosion must be completely telegraphed: visibly thinner edges, an on-screen erosion warning, and a hard rule that you cannot lose land banked more than N seconds ago. Aggression toward players is unforgivable; a visible clock they chose to ignore is fair.

Failure 4 — water feels mushy

Grid-locked boats feel crisp. Water-simulated boats feel like syrup. TIDE stays on the grid and stays snapped. Any smooth-steering experiment is a trap, however good it feels in the first ten seconds.

Failure 5 — mobile

Water animation plus territory redraw plus trail, at 60fps, on a four-year-old Android, is not free. This is our single largest technical risk.

Failure 6 — skill compression

A global tide hits everyone simultaneously. During FLOOD, noobs are safer and pros are nerfed — the skill gap flattens exactly when it should be sharpest. This is inherent to a global clock and cannot be fully solved. The only real mitigation is making FLOOD a steering test, which punishes bad players harder than good players' opponents do. Accepted as a known cost, not designed away.

Failure 7 — bots that teach the wrong lesson

A dumb bot makes the game feel empty. An unfair bot makes it feel broken. Bots must be beatable-looking: they must visibly commit to a mistake you could have predicted.

Failure 8 — scope

Tide + erosion + current + surge + exposed land is five systems. The prototype must ship none of them. This requires discipline, not optimism.

Section verdict

The mechanic as briefed is weak. Sharpened as in section 01, with the three specific commitments — the tide is a bet, the tide is a global overlay, erosion is telegraphed — it is strong. Recommendation: proceed to Phase 1 with the sharpened version, subject to the kill-test.

07 Technical risk register

RiskSeverityMitigation
Territory fill cost. Flood-fill on capture, multiplied by player count, plus erosion every tick. Naive implementation is a hard death. CRITICAL Capture uses a scanline flood fill restricted to the enclosed region. Erosion is a border-only pass — iterate owned cells adjacent to non-owned flooded cells. No flood at all on the hot path.
Fixed-step simulation. Render coupled to logic means erosion rate varies by machine. CRITICAL Simulation on a fixed 10 Hz tick, decoupled from render. Every rate is expressed per-tick, never per-frame. Non-negotiable from the first commit.
Mobile performance. Per-tile Graphics rebuilds will melt a phone. HIGH Territory drawn into a RenderTexture, redrawn on a dirty-rect basis. Water is one full-screen shader/graphics layer. DPR capped at 2.
Phaser Arcade Physics is the wrong tool. It wants continuous bodies; we want grid-locked kinematics. HIGH Manual integration. No Arcade, no Matter. Document this in the README so nobody helpfully adds it in month three.
State serialization. Netcode arrives in Beta; retro-fitting authority retroactively is brutal. MEDIUM The entire world state is a grid plus a player array. Keep it plain and serialisable from day one, even with a single local bot. Costs nothing now; saves weeks in Beta.
Trail memory. Naive trails leak. LOW Trail is an array of cell indices per player, capped, cleared on death.

08 Architecture

Your system list is right, and I would resist anything larger. Two additions I'd insist on, both small:

Additions to the specified list

  • Grid — owns the elevation/depth field and passability queries. It is data plus one function, not a system and not a framework. Without it, tide logic leaks into Player, Bot, and TerritorySystem simultaneously.
  • SimClock — the fixed 10 Hz tick. Not an ECS, not a scheduler. Just the thing that makes erosion deterministic across machines.

Everything else exactly as specified: GameScene, Player, Bot, TrailSystem, TerritorySystem, UI.

Explicitly rejected: dependency injection, plugin systems, ECS, microservices, entity-component systems, manager-within-manager patterns. If a file needs an interface, we write the interface when the second implementation exists — not before.

09 Roadmap

Phase 1 — Prototype
Smallest playable thing

Fixed hand-placed map. One human, one bot. Movement, trail, capture, death, respawn, round score, minimal HUD. Zero tide.

We validate the base loop, our grid code, and our controls before layering the risky mechanic on top. If capture does not feel good, tide will not save it.

Exit criteriaA stranger understands and captures land within 60 seconds, dies on their own intent, and asks for another round.
Phase 2 — MVP
Add the tide

Tide clock, water level, depth-based passability, erosion on flood, Surge multiplier, current field, 3-minute round, touch controls. Bot updated to respect the tide.

Exit criteriaKill-test passes — players expand on flood deliberately. Legibility test passes — players state the tide phase within 2 seconds of looking at the screen. 60fps on a mid-range phone.
Phase 3 — Alpha
Make it a game

Five bot archetypes — Expander, Ambusher, Farmer, Tide-Timer, Opportunist. Map seeds. Art and audio pass. A self-teaching first 15 seconds. Quality and colourblind settings. Local 2P.

Exit criteriaNo single bot strategy dominates. Players must visibly adapt between rounds.
Phase 4 — Beta
The hard one

Server-authoritative netcode, matchmaking, persistence and progression, anti-cheat baseline, low-end mobile performance, PWA, analytics and crash reporting, store page, load testing.

Exit criteria500 concurrent with zero desync. Crash-free above 99.5%. p95 join under 8 seconds.
Phase 5 — Launch
Go live

Store presence, creator push, leaderboards, seasons, live balance loop driven by telemetry.

Success metricsD1 retention above 35%. Median session above 6 minutes. D7 above 12%.

GitHub-first workflow

main is stable-only and branch-protected. All work goes feature/* → commit → push → PR → development. CI runs on every PR: typecheck, build, and a headless simulation smoke test. No direct pushes to main, ever, including hotfixes — hotfixes go fix/* → PR → main → back-merge to development.

10 Alternatives, if the tide still reads weak

Three stronger-in-a-specific-direction alternatives in the same .io territory-control genre, provided so the choice is deliberate rather than defaulted into.

NameCore changeStronger atWeaker at
A — LATTICE You claim by connecting cells you already own into a spanning structure, not by enclosing. Surgical, low-area, deliberate. Depth and mastery. A genuinely high skill ceiling; each capture is a small precise act rather than one big greedy loop. Onboarding and mobile legibility. Newcomers will not read this in 60 seconds, and a sparse web of cells is harder to parse on a phone than a solid blob.
B — WILDFIRE Claimed cells decay continuously and must be re-fed. Circling becomes defense. Tension and aggression. Constant pressure, no safe parking, high drama. Tone. It can read as stressful rather than fun, and the optimal play is genuinely hard to discover without a tutorial we have promised not to build.
C — STRATA You claim by descending. Each layer down is richer, and the surface above collapses behind you. Novelty and a fresh risk axis. Depth becomes the resource, and retreat is impossible. Legibility and camera. Depth is hard to render readably, and it fights the flat top-down view that territory games depend on entirely.
Ranking

Sharpened TIDE > LATTICE > STRATA > WILDFIRE

TIDE wins because it inherits Paper.io's proven legibility and adds exactly one idea, whereas each alternative replaces the core verb and inherits onboarding risk we would have to pay off later. LATTICE is the one I would revisit first if playtests show TIDE's ceiling is too shallow.

11 Phase 0 gate

Open decisions — needed before any code is written

  • Mechanic direction. Sharpened TIDE, or one of the three alternatives.
  • Theme and art direction. Undecided. This matters more than it looks: the fiction is what makes erosion feel fair. If territory is "what you have charted," decay is the tide taking your work back. If it is abstract territory, erosion reads as theft.

Also worth settling early, though they do not block Phase 1: whether Phase 1 ships the fixed map or a seeded one, and whether local 2P is a Phase 3 feature or a control on day one.

Standing constraint for Phase 1

Phase 1 contains no tide, no current, no erosion, no surge, and no art beyond functional colour. If a tide system appears in a Phase 1 branch, it does not merge. The prototype's only job is to prove that our capture loop feels good in our codebase, on our controls, before we build a mechanic that depends on it.
